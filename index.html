Michael A. Nelson · FILM PRACTITIONER

I'm a developing filmmaker from the Queensland University of Technology (QUT) interested in the intersection of film, music and storytelling. I’m drawn to the way cinematography, lighting, sound, editing and music can work together to communicate emotion, explore ideas and create stories that resonate with an audience.

I enjoy taking a project from its initial concept through to a finished piece, balancing creative decisions with the practical problem-solving needed to bring a vision to life. My interests and experience span directing, cinematography, lighting, sound recording, music production and post-production, with a particular focus on how editing, sound and visual composition shape an audience’s experience.

Through my work on The Fisherman's Daughter, I contributed as Continuity / Script Supervisor and Assistant Editor. I want to build a career in music production and video editing, while continuing to develop as a Writer and Director. I’m especially interested in music videos, narrative short films and documentaries that combine strong storytelling with thoughtful visual and sound design.

05 / Professional reflection

What the process taught me.

A critical account around my experience working on The Fisherman's Daughter

My Experience

reflective practice

ChallengesDecisionsCollaborationGrowth

Role and responsibilities

On The Fisherman's Daughter, I worked as Continuity / Script Supervisor and Assistant Editor. These roles connected my interest in storytelling with the practical processes that help a production move from filming into post-production. I am particularly interested in how editing, sound and visual composition influence an audience’s emotional response, so contributing to both continuity and editorial work helped me understand how production details support a coherent final film.

My primary responsibility as continuity supervisor was to record the shots and sound files captured during filming. I documented which shots were printable and their corresponding sound files, providing useful references for the director and editor. I also monitored prop positioning, fishing equipment and footprints in the sand during scenes involving walking on the beach. For example, I checked that footprints appeared in the correct sequence and did not appear before the story’s action created them. Similarly, I ensured fishing equipment was put down and picked up consistently in the same spots between different shot types.

I also worked with the director to create a trailer for the film. This involved considering shot selection, pacing, sound and narrative information to communicate the film’s identity without revealing too much. I collaborated with the director to preserve his creative vision and with the main editor to achieve a similar editing style. Together, these responsibilities connected accurate production documentation with the creative presentation of the film.

A challenge and how I responded

One challenge was a scheduling conflict during two filming days, when I had back-to-back rehearsals for a theatre production. To maintain continuity coverage, I coordinated with the Second Assistant Director to undertake continuity responsibilities during my absence. I provided my workflow to keep the continuity sheets consistent, then used the notes they supplied to finalise the daily reports.

This experience demonstrated the importance of documentation that another crew member can understand and continue using. Continuity systems should not depend entirely on one person’s presence. In future productions, I would prepare a more formal handover package, including reporting conventions and a checklist of important details to monitor.

Another challenge involved access to an additional storage device, primarily the data wrangler’s responsibility. I offered my own drive, but it was already being used by a previous collaborator who was unavailable to return it. I could not provide it as planned. Although I do not know the exact solution, the data wrangler communicated the issue to the producers, who addressed it. This reinforced the importance of communicating resource constraints early rather than relying on a workaround that may not be possible.

Continuity as a creative and technical practice

This production helped me understand how continuity contributes to the audience’s experience. Inconsistencies in props, movement or the environment can distract viewers, particularly when footage from different times or angles is combined. Monitoring the props, costumes and the natural set changes required me to consider not only whether details matched between shots, but also whether they appeared at the correct point in the story.

My shot and sound records were equally important. Marking printable shots and linking them with corresponding sound files created a practical reference for post-production. The continuity documentation included in my evidence gallery demonstrates how systematic observation can help editors identify and evaluate useful material.

Having previously worked with continuity supervisors whose notes were incomplete or difficult to read, I was particularly conscious of making my reports detailed, legible and useful. I wanted the director and editor to understand the information without interpreting unclear shorthand or reconstructing missing details. This reinforced my belief that effective continuity work depends not simply on recording information, but on communicating it clearly enough to support the wider production.

Collaboration and editorial workflow

Working across production and post-production highlighted the importance of collaboration. During filming, my records needed to communicate continuity information accurately. During trailer production, my focus shifted towards how the film would be presented to an audience, requiring consideration of its intended tone and the arrangement of footage.

Working with the main editor also helped me recognise that an editing style involves more than selecting appealing shots. Pacing, transitions, timing and the relationship between images and sound all influence how a piece feels. I learned to balance creative decisions with the director’s intentions and the established style of the project.

In future projects, I would establish clearer editorial review stages, allowing time to assess structure and pacing before refining individual details. This would help incorporate feedback efficiently while maintaining a shared creative direction.

Professional growth and future goals

My most significant professional development was learning to perform continuity supervision to a standard I believe was highly effective for the director and editor. I pursued this skill because I had experienced the difficulties caused by incomplete or illegible continuity reports. On The Fisherman’s Daughter, I applied those lessons by developing a workflow focused on clarity, accuracy and practical usefulness.

The production strengthened my attention to detail, organisational skills and understanding of how on-set decisions affect post-production. Managing a continuity handover taught me the value of transferable systems, while the trailer process gave me further experience collaborating on a film’s creative presentation.

Moving forward, I want to continue refining my continuity reports, develop more formal handover procedures and gain experience editing narrative projects and trailers. I also want to improve my understanding of media management so I can anticipate workflow constraints and respond constructively to technical challenges. Overall, The Fisherman’s Daughter helped me recognise how careful documentation and creative editorial work serve the same purpose: supporting a coherent film while preserving the intentions of the people making it.
